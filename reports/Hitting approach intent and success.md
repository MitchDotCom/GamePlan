# Capture intent first, then measure development

**GamePlan should log a coach's intent as a short, pre-filled, editable plan written before the game, and it should judge success on process and behavior over many games, not on results or on agreement with the model.** No public template for a hitter-intent log exists in baseball or in any other sport the researchers searched, so the schema below is a design proposal assembled from analogs, not a copy of proven practice. The strongest transferable idea is "log, then score": record the plan before the event, grade execution against it separately from the result (football assignment grading, forecasting scores, poker's "resulting"). The measurement ceiling is low. Under the researchers' own assumption-based calculation, a hitter with about 300 plate appearances (PA) cannot show a change in chase rate smaller than roughly 9 to 13 percentage points within a season, so outcome gains cannot be shown for a single Single-A hitter in one season, and the first-season claim should rest on adoption plus behavior. The usability record points the same way: documented reasons analytics tools get ignored are extra work, analyst vocabulary, information overload and poor timing, so the tool must add no work, use the coach's words, and fit the post-batting-practice meeting, the dugout glance and the postgame video session. **Evidence caveat that applies to everything below:** none of the six researchers could open full web pages (the network proxy blocked fetches). Nearly every external claim rests on a search-result snippet and is **unverified**. Only Baseball Savant pages were read directly. Claims are tagged [S] (snippet only, unverified) or [P] (page read in full). A "pages to read in full" list closes the report. Where a claim has no URL in the notes, it is omitted or labeled as the researchers' inference.

## No public intent template exists, so GamePlan has to define a minimal one

Public baseball sources show three kinds of artifact and none of them records a hitter's intended plan as a data field. The Red Sox pitchers-and-catchers meeting uses a sheet of hitter tendencies (results by handedness, pitch-type performance, 0-0 tendencies, swing-and-miss zones) that players can annotate ([Boston Globe, 2023-07-03](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2023/07/03/sports/an-inside-look-how-red-sox-game-plan-attack-opposing-hitters)) [S]. The Orioles send hitters an advance scouting report and possible attack plans by WhatsApp the night before, then meet individually after batting practice, and dropped large group hitter meetings because some players sat in the back and did not speak ([The Banner](https://www.thebanner.com/sports/orioles-mlb/orioles-hitting-meetings-individual-approach-FLG3E7UP2REEBFCGRM3PRC5VSI/)) [S]. A Red Sox affiliate hitting coach posting lists "Deliver Advanced Reports and Game Plans to hitters" and carrying out "individual player plans" as duties, which implies a written artifact at that club ([Red Sox posting](https://jobs.lever.co/redsox/edc90b80-7e24-4253-bd62-b0a1d352e9a0)) [S]. Everything fielded is tendency data; the "what to hunt and what to do with it" layer was not found in any public template. That absence is an inference from search snippets and is not proof that clubs lack such documents privately.

Vocabulary is shared at the count level and idiosyncratic beyond it. Count labels (first pitch, early count, two-strike) recur everywhere. Nationals coordinator Troy Gingrich required in 2016 that every hitter have "a plan and a two-strike approach", at minimum choking up ([MLB.com](https://www.mlb.com/phillies/news/how-juan-soto-developed-his-two-strike-batting-stance)) [S]. "Hunt", "attack plan" and "game plan" appear in club postings and quotes, but no published taxonomy or inter-club consistency study was found, and Dave Magadan called "tunneling" "probably just another word to get a good pitch to hit" ([Arizona Sports](https://arizonasports.com/mlb/arizona-diamondbacks/d-backs-hitting-coach-magadan-gm-hazen-discuss-teams-hitting-approach)) [S]. The design consequence is plain labels the coach can edit, not a fixed taxonomy. The target club's approach is mostly verbal today, and the Orioles pattern (written report, verbal commitment) suggests the written plan is an input while the real commitment happens in conversation. A tool therefore has to capture what the coach said in one tap or one short phrase, not demand a written plan.

Input-burden evidence is indirect. A systematic review of dietary self-monitoring (22 studies) found adherence declines over time ([PMC6856872](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6856872)) [S], and participants who found logging easier kept it up longer ([PMC7593856](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7593856)) [S]. A six-month smartphone-diary study reported 62% adherence on smartphones versus 34% on paper ([Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/52730-tracking-diet-smartphones-methods.html)) [S]. These are diet studies, not baseball, and may not transfer to a few-second dugout entry. Nothing was found on voice capture either way. Plan for decay over a long season: pre-filled defaults, one-tap entry, optional free text, and post-hoc capture during postgame video.

### Recommended minimal log schema (design proposal, not a published standard)

The existing record is keyed `date|starter|hitter|time-through-order|count` with `path`, `why`, `note` and a save time. The proposal keeps that per-count record and adds one game-level plan per hitter-and-starter, the unit on which every downstream measure hangs. Everything except the two bold fields is optional, and all but the coach's words can be auto-filled.

| Field | Type | Default | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| plan_id | string | auto | Joins pick, review and trend; missing today |
| game_date, starter_id, hitter_id | date, id, id | from context | Unit of plan |
| author_id | id | signed-in coach or device owner | Missing today (no coach identity) |
| model_stamp | string | auto | Which build was on screen |
| paths_shown | list of path ids | auto | What the coach chose among |
| **chosen_path** | enum: value, contact_first, decide_every_pitch, own, none | the path flagged as default | The one required tap |
| chosen_source | enum: accepted_default, picked_other, edited | auto | Separates acceptance from override |
| **intent_text** | string, 140 chars max | empty | Coach's own words; "My own plan" captures nothing today |
| focus_tags | up to 2 from an editable list (first-pitch hunt, take until strike, two-strike shorten, hunt pitch or zone) | none | Count-level vocabulary only |
| override_reason | enum: matchup_read, hitter_feel, pitcher_info, other | null; asked only on override | Structured reasons beat free text in the clinical override literature [S] |
| dev_goal_id | id | null | Links plan to a standing hitter goal (see below) |
| confidence | ordinal 1 to 3 | null | Optional probabilistic commitment, forecasting style; speculative |
| shared_with_hitter | bool | false | Adoption signal, handoff check |
| captured_when | enum: pregame, dugout, postgame_backfill | auto from time and device | Lets analysis exclude after-the-fact entries |
| created_at, locked_at | timestamps | auto | Pre-first-pitch lock makes the plan a commitment |

The per-count record keeps `count`, `path` and a boolean `changed_in_game`, and gains the `plan_id` join. A standing hitter-level goal record (`dev_goal_id`, one sentence such as "stop chasing sliders below the zone", a metric from the process list below, a start date) is the missing link from plan to development. Locking the plan at first pitch matters because the football, forecasting and chess analogs only work when the commitment predates the result. Backfill entries should be allowed but flagged and excluded from adherence statistics.

## Success in three layers, and what each layer can and cannot say

### Adoption: measure it, but do not call it success

Adoption metrics are the only layer a single club can read within weeks, and they are hypotheses, not validated predictors. The notes found no sports-tech retention study linking opens, picks, overrides, returns or sharing to continued use. The closest evidence is clinical decision support: in a systematic review of trials, features that predicted improved practice were automatic provision within the workflow, recommendations rather than assessments, provision at the time and place of the decision, and computer-based delivery, with 30 of 32 systems having all four succeeding ([Kawamoto, BMJ 2005](https://www.bmj.com/content/330/7494/765)) [S]. Alert acceptance fell with work complexity and repeats, with each extra reminder per encounter cutting acceptance by about 30% ([Ancker 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5387195/)) [S]. Override rate alone is ambiguous: a systematic review reports clinical override rates of 46.2% to 96.2% with 29.4% to 100% of overrides judged appropriate depending on alert type ([JMIR Med Inform 2020](https://medinform.jmir.org/2020/7/e15653)) [S]. The candidate indicators, all researcher hypotheses, are: share of scheduled games with a plan locked before first pitch, returns across consecutive games, share of plans with at least one edit (engagement, not failure), plan opened in the dugout after pregame prep, and share shared with the hitter. Reliability is unknown for all of them. Show them to the coach as a plain weekly line ("plans locked 5 of 6 games, 2 shared with hitters") with no score. What not to claim: that adoption shows the tool works, or that a high or low override rate is good or bad.

### Behavior and process: the layer where evidence can accumulate

The question here is whether the hitter did what the plan said, and whether decisions (not results) improved. Published reliability is thin and inconsistent. Carleton's convention calls a rate stabilized when split-half correlation reaches r = 0.70 ([FanGraphs](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/a-new-way-to-look-at-sample-size)) [S]. Reported stabilization points conflict across vintages: K% at 60 PA in one FanGraphs-attributed summary ([FanGraphs](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/randomness-stabilization-regression)) [S] versus swing% at 50 PA, contact% at 100 PA and K% at 150 PA in a summary of a Baseball Prospectus article ([BP](https://www.baseballprospectus.com/?p=14215)) [S]. These are PA-based, whereas chase and zone contact use smaller denominators and probably need more PA, an inference no source confirmed. No published reliability figure was found for Statcast swing/take run value ([Baseball Savant swing-take leaderboard](https://baseballsavant.mlb.com/leaderboard/swing-take)) [P for definition], SEAGER, SwRV or PLV Decision Value, whose predictive claims are author claims ([Down on the Farm](https://downonthefarm.substack.com/p/a-closer-look-at-swing-decisions); [Pitcher List](https://pitcherlist.com/examining-hitters-combining-selectivity-with-aggression-using-plv-dv/)) [S]. These models also carry model error on top of sampling error.

The minimum detectable change figures below are **assumption-based calculations by the researchers, not published figures**. They assume independent pitches, two-sided alpha 0.05, 80% power, equal n in baseline and post periods, and league-typical base rates the researchers assumed without a source (chase 30%, zone swing 68%, zone contact 84%, whiff per swing 25%, overall swing 46%). Overdispersion from pitcher and count mix makes true values larger.

| Metric (assumed base rate) | n per period | Detectable change, percentage points |
|---|---|---|
| Chase (30%) | 100 / 200 / 400 out-of-zone pitches | 18.1 / 12.8 / 9.1 |
| Zone swing (68%) | 100 / 200 pitches | 18.5 / 13.1 |
| Zone contact (84%) | 60 / 150 / 300 zone swings | 18.7 / 11.9 / 8.4 |
| Whiff per swing (25%) | 100 / 200 / 400 swings | 17.1 / 12.1 / 8.6 |
| Overall swing (46%) | 300 / 600 / 1,000 pitches | 11.4 / 8.1 / 6.2 |

With an assumed 3.9 pitches per PA, 300 PA is about 1,150 pitches, about 520 out of the zone, so a half-season split gives roughly 260 out-of-zone pitches per period and a chase threshold near 11 points. Against a long, well-estimated prior baseline the same assumptions give a chase threshold of 11.4, 8.1 and 5.7 points at 100, 200 and 400 out-of-zone pitches. Decision-value run metrics have no variance data, so no figure can be derived.

The design response is to show the coach shrunk estimates with ranges, not raw rates. Empirical-Bayes beta-binomial shrinkage beat raw early-season batting averages in Efron and Morris's classic result, as described by a secondary explainer ([metricgate](https://metricgate.com/calculator/hierarchical-beta-binomial-baseball)) [S], and Bayesian multilevel single-case models showed better Type I error control in small samples in a simulation ([UNED](https://portalcientifico.uned.es/documentos/69f8dc6a579ceb0ec2bf3d2f)) [S]. For causal claims the notes favor a stepped or multiple-baseline rollout staggered across hitters, which sports science uses in single-case designs ([PMC12194246](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12194246/)) [S], and note that sport-psychology single-case studies often suffer publication bias and missing multiple baselines ([Staffordshire](https://eprints.staffs.ac.uk/6381/1/Accepted%20Version%20MRA%20in%20Sport%20Psychology_PSE%20June%202020.pdf)) [S]. No baseball-development example of a stepped rollout was found. About eight or more hitters would be needed for useful leverage, a researcher inference. Control charts (weekly chase, for example) work as drift alerts, not proof.

What to show a coach here: a per-hitter row with the plan's one-sentence goal, adherence across the last several games, and one process metric tied to the goal with a range and a "thin data" badge until a pre-set pitch count is reached (about 200 out-of-zone pitches per period for chase, from the table). Pair chase with in-zone swing and contact quality, because the Goodhart risk is real: a hitter told to cut chase may swing less everywhere, take more hittable pitches and post walks without damage. That failure mode is the researchers' inference; the Goodhart commentary found is general ([HoopVision, 2020](https://hoopvision.substack.com/p/incentive-stats-and-goodharts-law)) [S], with no baseball-specific study.

### Outcome: context only

Wins, OPS and wOBA in one season at Single-A are too noisy for attribution; the thresholds above show that even process rates barely move the needle in 300 PA. Show outcomes as context beside the process row, never as the verdict.

### What not to claim

Do not claim the tool caused any change in a hitter's results. Do not present model-grade agreement as development (the current review does). Do not quote the stabilization points as established, because they conflict and are snippet-level. Do not use "decision value" scores as validated measures of decision quality, because no peer-reviewed validation was found. Do not tie playing time to any single metric. Do not read a bad result on a good plan as a failure; Annie Duke's "resulting" names exactly this error ([Annie Duke](https://www.annieduke.com/?p=3108)) [S].

## Coaches ignore tools that add work, speak analyst, or arrive late

The documented reasons are communication and usability problems more than distrust. Front-office data tools are not easily usable by coaches and players, and the two groups "speak two different languages", a theme from an MIT Sloan panel summary ([MIT News, 2018](https://news.mit.edu/2018/mit-sloan-sports-analytics-conference-explores-data-share-0226)) [S]. Overloading athletes with data too early slows buy-in, a vendor claim ([Rapsodo](https://rapsodo.com/blogs/baseball/the-ultimate-rapsodo-playbook-for-coaches-build-better-players-smarter-sessions-and-stronger-recruiting-profiles)) [S]. South Carolina's player-development director described early TrackMan use as "trying to drink water out of a fire hose" ([UofSC, 2019](https://www.sc.edu/uofsc/posts/2019/02/precision_baseball.php)) [S]. Interview studies of football coaches cite limited expertise, too little time for interpretation, cost, cultural resistance and the mathematical nature of complex metrics ([DFB record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/dfb/Record/4091373)) [S]; the Frontiers 2021 review lists accelerators and inhibitors of analytics adoption ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.671601/text)) [S]. Players have said front-office communication did not match what they felt should be done ([Inquirer, 2016](https://www.inquirer.com/philly/sports/phillies/20160426_Phillies_trying_to_balance_data_and_instincts.html)) [S]. Most of these sources are pre-2019, and the notes found no named-staff account of why a specific tablet product was dropped, so this is a pattern across general sources, not a documented autopsy.

Two bodies of evidence bear on trust. Experts tend to under-use good models after seeing them err: people lose confidence in algorithms faster than in humans after the same error ([Dietvorst et al. 2015](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/20-Dietvorst-Simmons-Massey-2015.pdf)) [S], and experienced forecasters relied less on algorithmic advice than lay people ([Logg et al. 2019](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v151y2019icp90-103.html)) [S]. Letting users modify the output, even slightly, raised willingness to use it ([Dietvorst et al. 2018](https://repository.upenn.edu/marketing_papers/389)) [S]. In the opposite direction, automation bias leads clinicians to follow wrong advice ([Goddard et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751)) [S], and cognitive forcing functions cut overreliance but were rated least favorably ([Buçinca et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.09692)) [S]. Baseball variance makes a visible per-at-bat miss certain, so the reasoning is: make the plan editable (the best-supported reuse lever), keep any forcing step for the pregame laptop and out of the dugout, and judge the plan over many PA. Uncertainty displays have mixed support: confidence scores helped calibrate trust but did not improve joint accuracy ([Zhang et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.02114)) [S], while uncertainty estimates in weather forecasts improved decisions ([Joslyn and LeClerc 2012](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/items/5005f5c9-f791-4882-84fd-5ec28581d181/full)) [S]. Coarse "thin data" flags are the cautious choice and should be tested.

Time and device reality for Single-A is largely inferred. A minor-league hitting coach reviews the opposing starter and the previous night's video before noon, players arrive about 1:45 p.m., and an offensive meeting after batting practice and glove work sets the game plan, per one feature (a High-A Dayton Dragons profile; date unconfirmed) ([Journal-News](https://www.journal-news.com/sports/dayton-dragons-meet-the-hit-doctor-behind-the-reds-next-generation/article_a4d8fd6e-b1dd-5052-a992-5a2e45816468.html)) [S]. No source gave a minute-level time budget for dugout look-ups, the pregame meeting or postgame video. The inference for GamePlan: the laptop view serves the pre-noon desk time (heavy review) and the post-batting-practice meeting (the single group moment), the tablet serves a seconds-long glance, and postgame video is the place for backfill. On format, expert-written text beat standard graphs for clinical staff's decisions ([BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/hospital-staff-make-better-decisions-using-textual-information-rather-medical)) [S], and another summary says graphics help find critical items while text helps extract exact values ([unnamed search result; underlying paper not identified]). That is non-baseball evidence, so the proposal is a hypothesis: one plain sentence in the coach's words per hitter, plus one small zone graphic. No baseball heat-map color convention was found; do not assert one. For handoff, practitioner commentary says the coach is the translator, and wording should be the coach's own ([The Hitting Vault](https://thehittingvault.com/?p=5700)) [S].

Outdoor tablet use rests on generic standards: Apple's 44x44 pt touch target and WCAG 2.2 AA's 24x24 CSS px minimum ([Front-End Checklist](https://frontendchecklist.io/rules/accessibility/touch-targets)) [S], 4.5:1 body-text contrast ([Passiro](https://passiro.com/web-accessibility/developers/mobile-accessibility)) [S], and vendor notes that sunlight readability depends on glare and contrast, and that cold and gloves degrade capacitive touch ([E3 Displays](https://www.e3displays.com/reads/capacitive-vs.-resistive-technology-in-sunlight-readable-touch-screens)) [S]. Build well above minimums and test on the actual tablet in sun.

The practical readings are these. If a human umpire calls the zone, the take side of the called-strike model stays valid, and the 2026 plate-location convention change must be handled in the data adapter (the code review flags this assumption as unverified for Single-A). The pitch clock matters too: a 2026 rules summary says Single-A allows no batter timeouts except special circumstances, which shortens between-pitch time ([MLB.com](https://www.mlb.com/news/new-rule-changes-coming-to-minor-leagues-in-2026)) [S]. Because public data excludes the California League, production use needs a club-supplied feed, which also resolves the data-terms question. The safest dugout design is pre-loaded, offline, and shows only the plan the coach wrote; a live pitch-by-pitch recommender would look like the thing MLB just restricted, although MiLB applicability is unconfirmed and must be asked of the club.

## Questions for a 20-minute coach interview

1. Walk me through yesterday: when did you first look at the opposing starter, what did you look at, and what did you write down, if anything?
2. In the post-batting-practice meeting, what do you actually tell each hitter, and in what words? Can you give me two real examples from last week?
3. Is there one hitter whose goal this month you can state in one sentence? How would you know it's working?
4. During the game, when do you look at anything on a device, for how many seconds, and what do you look for? What does the club allow in the dugout, and does anything in the rules concern recommendations or Wi-Fi?
5. If I handed you a tablet right now, what is the one thing you would want to tap, and what should never need typing?
6. Which of these words do you and your staff use the same way (hunt, take until strike, two-strike approach, situational, work the count) and which differ by coach?
7. Tell me about the last tool, spreadsheet or app you stopped using. What made you stop?
8. When a hitter does what you asked and makes an out, how do you talk about it afterward? When he ignores the plan and gets a hit?
9. Do hitters see a written plan today? What would you not want a hitter to see?
10. During postgame video, how much time is there, and who runs it? Would you fix labels there?
11. How does your club treat umpire inconsistency, and are any California League parks using challenge or tracking systems you know of?
12. What would make you not use this tool again in the first month? What would convince your coordinator it is worth keeping?

## Conclusion

The central surprise is that the hard problem is not modelling but commitment: no sport has a public example of a coach's pre-game plan logged and graded afterward, so GamePlan's decision log is the product's differentiator, and it earns that role only if it locks the plan before first pitch, records the coach's own words, and grades the hitter against that plan separately from both the result and the model. A second shift is in what the first season can honestly claim. Because even the researchers' optimistic assumptions put the detectable change in chase at roughly 9 to 13 points for a single hitter, development claims must come from behavior against stated goals, shown as shrunk estimates with ranges, with outcomes labeled as context.

What remains open is mostly local: whether California League parks have any tracking, whether MiLB mirrors MLB's 2026 dugout restriction, and how the target club's coaches would actually use five seconds in the dugout. The 20-minute interview and a full read of the listed pages should settle these before any schema field, label name or metric is fixed.
