# Adoption, trust and evaluation of decision-support tools by expert users (sports coaching focus)

Method note: research run 2026-10-04. Several publisher domains (Frontiers, McKinsey, MLB.com, JMIR) were blocked by the network proxy, so those items rest on search-result summaries, not full-text reads, and are flagged "snippet only". Tiers: A peer-reviewed/primary data, B named practitioner account, C vendor/marketing, D opinion. Dates are publication dates.

## 1. Documented sports cases of adoption, ignoring or abandonment, and stated reasons

### Takeaway
Sports evidence on why coaches ignore analytics is mostly qualitative (interviews, practitioner accounts). Recurrent stated reasons: time, staffing, statistical complexity, cultural resistance, and weak translation from front office to coaches to players. I found no rigorous before/after study of a single analytics tool's adoption in baseball dugouts.

### Cited Findings
- [A, snippet only] A Frontiers in Sports and Active Living paper (2021) examined analytics in player decisions by general managers and coaches and lists accelerators and inhibitors of adoption; it reports "massive resistance" from coaches/managers reported in the literature. — [Frontiers, 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.671601/text)
- [A, snippet only] Collegiate soccer coaches: poster on barriers and facilitators to adopting performance analytics data. — [UWF poster](https://ircommons.uwf.edu/esploro/outputs/eventposter/Barriers-and-Facilitators-to-the-Adoption/99381555074606600)
- [A, snippet only] Interview/thematic-analysis studies in football (Bundesliga academy, Danish coaches) cite limited expertise, insufficient time for interpretation, cost, cultural resistance, limited knowledge-sharing systems, and the mathematical nature of complex metrics as buy-in barriers; recommends coach-analyst co-development of tools. — search results aggregating [Danish coaches record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/dfb/Record/4076404?lng=en), [DFB records](https://lida.sport-iat.de/dfb/Record/4091373). Individual studies not verified; treat as directional.
- [B, snippet only] Astros GM (McKinsey blog) said buy-in took patience on the scouting side and the harder part was changing behavior of coaches and players. Date not retrieved. — [McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/people-and-organizational-performance/our-insights/the-organization-blog/houston-astros-winning-the-world-series-with-advanced-analytics)
- [B, snippet only] Angels: then-GM Billy Eppler reportedly grew frustrated that manager and coaches were not implementing data in games or communicating enough of it to players; MLB.com piece headlined as Eppler and Scioscia "on same page" (date not retrieved). — [MLB.com](https://www.mlb.com/news/billy-eppler-mike-scioscia-on-angels-analytics-c165460922)
- [B/D] Moneyball: in the film Art Howe is portrayed as resistant; Howe disputes this. Source describes Beane dictating lineups, so "adoption" there was top-down control rather than coach buy-in. Treat the Moneyball portrayal as contested. — [Art Howe, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Howe); [IBWAA Substack](https://ibwaa.substack.com/p/the-real-art-howe-nothing-like-the) (D)
- [B/D, snippet only] Rays under Joe Maddon: embraced analytics-guided strategy but Maddon also stressed fundamentals and "common sense", i.e. analytics adopted when the manager translated it into his own framing. — [Sportsnaut](https://sportsnaut.com/mlb/joe-maddon-speaks-out-about-analytics-angels) (D)
- [B, snippet only] Data overload: South Carolina baseball's player development director described early TrackMan use as "trying to drink water out of a fire hose"; TrackMan is used by all 174 MLB minor-league affiliates (per article). — [UofSC 2019](https://www.sc.edu/uofsc/posts/2019/02/precision_baseball.php)
- [A] NFL fourth down: Romer (JPE, 2006), 1,068 fourth-down situations; coaches did not go for it roughly 90% of the time when analysis favored it. Persistent non-adoption of an analytic recommendation by expert decision-makers. Disconfirming: critics argue coaches' behavior is consistent with optimization given job-security incentives (principal-agent), so "ignoring" may reflect incentives, not distrust. — [Romer JPE 2006](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~dromer/papers/JPE_April06.pdf); critique noted in search summary, no specific critic URL verified.
- Disconfirming/nuance: Wichita State coaches pass TrackMan info to staff and use "a good mix" of data and traditional views. — [The Sunflower](https://thesunflower.com/84051/sports/how-wichita-state-baseball-uses-data-and-analytics-for-player-development/) (D)

### Inferences
- Stated reasons map to product risks: time (dugout use must be seconds), vocabulary (metrics mathematically complex), the last-mile gap to players (output should be sharable in player-facing language), and incentive/blame structure.
- Successful accounts (Rays, Wichita State) describe a translator (manager/analyst) between data and players; the tool may need to serve as that translator.

### Gaps
- No full-text verified case of a minor-league (Single-A) hitting tool's adoption/abandonment. No named abandonment of a specific tablet dugout product found. Vendor adoption data (C) not found.
- Many cited sources were snippet-only; the report writer should not quote them as verified.

## 2. Algorithm aversion and automation bias: how experts respond, what raises appropriate reliance

### Takeaway
Experts often under-use good models (especially after seeing errors), yet in other settings over-rely on wrong advice. Letting users modify outputs reliably increases use; forcing deliberate engagement reduces over-reliance but is disliked.

### Cited Findings
- [A] Dietvorst, Simmons, Massey, JEP: General (2015): people lose confidence in algorithms faster than in humans after seeing the same error, and choose the human even when the algorithm is better. — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/20-Dietvorst-Simmons-Massey-2015.pdf)
- [A] Dietvorst et al., Management Science (2018): letting people modify an imperfect algorithm's forecast even slightly raised willingness to use it and satisfaction; insensitive to size of allowed modification. — [Penn repository](https://repository.upenn.edu/marketing_papers/389)
- [A] Logg, Minson, Moore, OBHDP (2019): lay people adhered more to algorithmic than human advice ("algorithm appreciation"), but experienced forecasters relied less on algorithmic advice than lay people, hurting accuracy. Disconfirms universal aversion; supports expert-specific resistance. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v151y2019icp90-103.html)
- [A] Burton, Stein, Jensen, J. Behav. Decis. Making (2020), systematic review of 61 articles: five themes for causes/solutions: expectations and expertise, decision autonomy, incentivization, cognitive compatibility, divergent rationalities. — [CBS repository](https://research.cbs.dk/en/publications/a-systematic-review-of-algorithm-aversion-in-augmented-decision-m/)
- [A] SSRN paper (2023/24) argues aversion vs appreciation is reconciled by hypothetical vs real judgments (details not verified). — [SSRN 4687557](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4687557)
- [A] Gaube et al., npj Digital Medicine (2021): radiologists rated advice lower when labeled AI; less-expert physicians did not; accuracy fell with inaccurate advice regardless of source. — [PMC7896064](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7896064)
- [A] Goddard, Roudsari, Wyatt, JAMIA (2012; online 2011), systematic review: automation bias yields commission errors (following wrong advice) and omission errors; CDS can introduce new errors even while improving average performance. — [PMC3240751](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751)
- [A] Buçinca, Malaya, Gajos, CSCW (2021), n=199: cognitive forcing functions reduced overreliance more than plain explanations, but users rated them least favorably; benefits larger for high Need for Cognition. — [arXiv 2102.09692](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.09692)

### Inferences
- For a coach, "adjust before accepting" (editable plan, swap targets) is the best-supported lever for reuse; forcing functions would likely hurt dugout adoption and fit pregame laptop better.
- Aversion applies mainly after visible misses: a hitting tool will often be "wrong" per at-bat (baseball variance), a high-risk setup for Dietvorst-type erosion. Frame recommendations probabilistically and judge over many PAs.

### Gaps
- No study of coaches (vs. MBA students, lay people, clinicians) specifically in the aversion paradigm. No evidence on the effect in repeated low-stakes tablet use.

## 3. Showing uncertainty or confidence

### Takeaway
Evidence is mixed: confidence helps calibration somewhat but does not guarantee better joint performance; uncertainty forecasts help non-experts decide and buffer against forecast error.

### Cited Findings
- [A] Zhang, Liao, Bellamy (FAT* 2020): confidence scores helped calibrate trust but calibration alone did not improve joint accuracy; depends on whether the human has complementary knowledge; local explanations were problematic. — [arXiv 2001.02114](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.02114)
- [A] Joslyn & LeClerc, JEP: Applied 18(1) (2012): uncertainty estimates in weather forecasts improved decisions and attenuated effects of forecast error and perhaps raised trust, though the best format depends on the situation. — [UW summary](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/items/5005f5c9-f791-4882-84fd-5ec28581d181/full)
- [A, snippet only] Zhang et al. 2020/2022 cited as showing linguistic uncertainty encourages cautious behavior; recent LLM work finds longer explanations raise user confidence without raising accuracy. — [arXiv 2401.13835](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2401.13835)
- [A, snippet only] AIES 2021 poster: stakeholders struggle to interpret uncertainty estimates. — [AIES poster](https://www.aies-conference.com/2021/wp-content/posters/74_%20Uncertainty%20as%20a%20Form%20of%20Transparency_%20Measuring%2C%20Communicating%2C%20and%20Using%20Uncertainty.pdf)

### Inferences
- Coarse labels (e.g. sample size / "thin data" flags) and ranges are safer than numeric probabilities in a dugout glance; test with logging (see Q6).

### Gaps
- No sports-specific study of uncertainty displays for coaches; no head-to-head of display formats for baseball hitters.

## 4. Adoption metrics predicting continued use

### Takeaway
I found no citable study linking opens/picks/overrides/returns/sharing to retention for sports-tech. Closest transferable evidence: workflow-integrated, recommendation-giving CDS predicts success.

### Cited Findings
- [A] Kawamoto et al., BMJ (2005), systematic review of trials: four features predicted improved practice: automatic provision within workflow, recommendations (not just assessments), provision at time/place of decision, computer-based; 30 of 32 systems with all four succeeded. Also supported: periodic performance feedback and requesting reasons for not following recommendations. — [BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/330/7494/765)
- [A] Ancker et al., BMC MIDM (2017): alert acceptance fell with work complexity and repeats, not workload; each extra reminder per encounter cut acceptance by ~30%; no desensitization over time for a new alert. — [PMC5387195](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5387195/)

### Inferences
- Candidate leading indicators (hypotheses, not evidence-backed): return across consecutive games, share of plans with at least one edit (engagement, not failure), plan viewed in dugout after pregame prep, share with players. Override rate alone is ambiguous (CDS shows high overrides can be rational).

### Gaps
- No public product-analytics or sports-tech retention study found; vendor (C) claims not located.

## 5. Override design: treating and learning from overrides

### Takeaway
CDS shows very high override rates, partly justified; structured, low-friction reasons plus reviewing override comments improve alerts. Repeating the same alert drives dismissal.

### Cited Findings
- [A] JMIR Med Inform systematic review (2020): average override rates 46.2%-96.2%; 29.4%-100% of overrides judged appropriate depending on alert type; recommends better alert types, clear information and rationale. — [JMIR 2020](https://medinform.jmir.org/2020/7/e15653) (snippet only)
- [A, snippet] A 2024 meta-analysis reports ~90% physician override average (95% CI 85-95%) per a secondary source; verify before quoting. — [Mindbowser](https://www.mindbowser.com/alert-fatigue-healthcare/) (C/D, secondary)
- [A, snippet] Common override reasons: "will monitor", "tolerated before", "not clinically significant", "benefit outweighs risk" (3 categories = 78%); menu-based reasons improved appropriateness; override comments are a rich data source for finding broken alerts. — [PMC6748816](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6748816); [Healthcare IT News](https://healthcareitnews.com/node/542433) (D)
- [A] Ancker 2017 (above): repeated alerts for same patient lowered acceptance. 

### Inferences
- Treat an override as data: capture one-tap reason (matchup read, hitter feel, pitcher info, other), do not re-nag for the same hitter/pitcher, and review override clusters weekly with coaches as product feedback. Do not auto-retrain on overrides without outcome checks, since overrides can be wrong (automation bias runs both ways).

### Gaps
- No evidence on override learning in sports tools; no evidence on how asking for override reasons affects dugout speed.

## 6. Evaluating a decision aid when randomization is impractical

### Takeaway
Use staggered rollout (stepped wedge, randomized if possible, else staggered by team/game) with logged exposure and behavior outcomes first, and treat outcome changes as secondary given baseball noise.

### Cited Findings
- [A] Stepped-wedge design: all clusters eventually get the intervention in randomly ordered steps; suited when parallel control is unethical or simultaneous rollout is impractical; risk of confounding with secular time trends. — [Hemming/Brown, Trials 2006 systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17092344/); [BMC 2006](https://www.biomedcentral.com/1471-2288/6/54)
- [A] CDS hybrid effectiveness-implementation stepped-wedge protocol example with RE-AIM evaluation. — [ClinicalTrials.gov protocol](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/91/NCT03374891/Prot_SAP_000.pdf)

### Inferences
- Chain of evidence: exposure (viewed) -> behavior change (plan followed: pitch-type/zone approach, swing decisions logged by staff) -> process outcomes (chase rate, swing decisions, contact quality) -> results (wOBA/OPS); Single-A samples are small, so lean on behavior and process measures. Pre-register comparisons; use within-hitter before/after with non-using teams or periods as control.

### Gaps
- No sports-specific stepped-rollout evaluation found; sample size/power calculations for Single-A hitting not sourced.
